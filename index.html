Where should
AI stop?

As AI takes on more of the work, which decisions should stay with people? I began with video editing, where I first learned how much judgment sits inside a single cut, and followed the question outward.

00:00Prologue

I came to this question through a camera.

Before I wrote code, I made images. I photographed dance and theatre in college, studied media in graduate school, worked in production for nine months at THIRTEEN/WNET in New York and for three months at Tencent Video, and wrote and directed a short film, It’s Just a Cold, which confronts depression stigma in East Asian communities and asks viewers to see depression just like a cold: anyone can catch it, there is nothing to fear, and it can be faced and cared for. I also wrote its song, “与自己对话” (Speaking to Myself), later released on QQ Music under my artist name, 安玥.

Editing taught me how many judgments sit inside one cut: where a shot begins, how long a silence holds, what the audience sees first. When AI began to make those cuts, I wanted to know which of them people would still want to make themselves.

00:12Question

Automation removes work. It can also remove decisions.

Generative video tools can now transcribe, clean up audio, assemble a rough cut and generate whole scenes. In public discussions of these tools, creators welcomed help with repetitive execution. They drew firmer lines around pacing, voice, story, style and the final call.

01:04Finding

People readily delegate effort. They are more selective about delegating judgment.

Where the line falls depends less on how much work AI does than on what kind of work it takes over.

01:48Boundary

What moves the boundary?

The same person may welcome heavy automation in one step and object to a single AI decision in the next. Four conditions recur.

Stakes

Small, reversible choices are easy to hand over. Choices with consequences, or that carry the creator’s identity, get closer scrutiny.

Editability

Imperfect output is acceptable when people can step in, fix one part and keep the rest.

Intent

Automation becomes sensitive once a decision carries pacing, tone, story, voice or style.

Authority

Trust breaks fastest when AI acts beyond what a person believes they asked for or approved.

How much AI automates is not the same as how much control people give up.

02:30Handoff

Control is negotiated at the handoffs.

The tension was rarely “AI or human”. It appeared at the moments of transfer: when AI starts acting, when a person needs to step in, and when the work becomes final.

03:16Implications

Design the handoff, not only the automation.

For generative and AI-assisted video tools, the opportunity may lie less in covering more of the work with AI and more in making each transfer between AI execution and human judgment deliberate.

Authorization

Ask before crossing the boundary.

Automate bounded clean-up with confidence. Actions that add cost, change creative intent or overwrite approved work need explicit consent.

Preservation

Keep what already works.

Let people lock approved elements and revise locally, instead of regenerating everything to fix one problem.

Judgment

Return the choices that matter.

Surface the few decisions that shape the work, such as the opening, pacing, tone and ending, rather than asking people to supervise every step.

AI removes decisions until a decision becomes worth making.
04:02Beyond video

Every product is now deciding how far AI should go.

The same question now runs through the technology industry. Teams are choosing which parts of their products AI should handle, and the default is often as much as possible.

Consider a platform that helps people find a therapist. It could add AI search. Then a chatbot that recommends a therapist. Then one that answers people while they wait, including people in distress. Each step is technically easy. The harder question is where the system should stop and hand the person to another person.

In a video editIn mental health care
StakesA weaker cut, which can be redoneA missed sign of risk, or a referral to the wrong kind of help
EditabilityRevise one clip and keep the restA conversation cannot be taken back
IntentThe creator’s pacing, voice and styleA person’s own decisions about their life and relationships
AuthorityDid I approve this edit?Did I agree to share this, and who will see it?

The four conditions still apply, with more at stake. In video, the boundary protects authorship. In care, it protects people. This is the principle I carried into MoodOwn: AI can meet people and help them think, but at the moments that matter it should stop and connect them with a person.

04:50In my own work

I have been moving the boundary in my own work, too.

When I began to build software, the question came with me. My answer has changed with each project.

  1. Handing it all to AI

    My first app, Leave1%, was built almost entirely by AI. It ran, but it was a black box: I could not fix it or tell whether it was safe.

  2. Generating, not directing

    When I tried to design an interface with AI, I could regenerate it again and again, but I could not direct it. I began asking which decisions I wanted back. That question became this study.

  3. Learning from AI, creating with AI

    On Into the Stars, the concept and the design were mine. AI helped me plan the architecture and taught me to build it. We reviewed the code together, and I asked it to explain what I could not yet read. When it could not fix one bug, I found the fix myself.

  4. Working with AI, staying in the loop

    Now I want real technical depth, so that I can work with AI as a team: guide it, check its work and stay in the loop, while the ideas, the purpose and my love of the world remain my own.

Where AI should stop is not a fixed line. It moves as people learn, and it should move by their choice.

That is why I am returning to computer science.
